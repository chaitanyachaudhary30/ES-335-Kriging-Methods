Predicting soil pH where nobody has sampled

Kriging and Gaussian processes are built on one idea: places close together have similar soil. We ask whether simple statistics of the data can tell us, before testing, which of these methods will work best in regions the model has never seen, and when none of them will beat a plain average.

Step 1. What are we trying to predict?

Soil can be acidic or alkaline, like the liquids below. This is measured as pH. Farmers care because most crops grow best between about pH 5.5 and 7, and acidic soil needs lime.

The catch: pH is measured by digging up soil and sending it to a lab. The European survey we use measured about 1,700 farms across an area of over 3 million km², about one farm per 1,900 km². For every other field we have to guess from the farms that were measured. Our project asks which way of guessing works best, especially for whole regions with no measurements at all.

Step 3. What happens when a whole region has no measurements?

Real maps have gaps: areas where no farm was sampled. Use the slider to remove every measurement inside a square in the middle of the country, then see how well each guess does inside that square.

Try it: make a gap

What to notice. In a small gap, smart averaging still helps, because there are measured farms nearby. As the gap grows, the help shrinks to zero: deep inside a large gap, kriging gives back the plain average. (This toy uses one known average for the whole country. Our real models estimate the average from the nearest samples instead, so far from the data they fall back to a local average, which in Europe can still beat the overall one; see Section 8.) Copying the nearest dot gets worse than the average, because a far-away farm is no better than a random one, and you also copy its quirks.

Step 4. Two properties of the soil decide how much guessing can help

Try it: change the soil

  • Similarity distance. Farms closer than this distance tend to have similar soil; farther apart they are unrelated. A short distance means patchy soil, and the dots stop helping sooner (the curve in Step 3 drops faster).
  • Random noise. Part of every measurement is pure chance: lab error, or a different patch of the same field. No method can predict chance. The dashed line in Step 3 shows the best any method could do; more noise pushes it down.

Experts call the similarity distance the range and the noise share the nugget ratio. For the real European data we measured both: soils stay related over about 500 km, and about 36% of the variation is noise. Sections 4 and 6 use these two numbers to predict, before testing, how well each method should do.

Step 5. A fair test hides whole regions

To score a method we hide some measured farms, guess them, and compare. How we hide them changes the score.

Same method, two tests

The usual habit in machine learning, hiding random rows, would make our methods look better than they really are for unmapped regions. That is why our main test hides whole 500 km regions of Europe (Section 5).

Step 6. What we found on real European soils

The short version

Using nearby measurements helps, but only up to a distance, and never beyond the limit set by random noise. In regions far from any measurement, the best methods were only about 20% better than guessing the average, and several quite different methods tied. Our main test failed: the statistics did not rank the methods correctly (the rank correlation reached the target of 0.7 in only 1 of 5 tests), and they predicted that kriging would improve on the average by about 10%, when it reached about 20%. The noise limit worked out before testing did hold. Only after testing did we find why the prediction was off: kriging's own error was predicted closely (0.93 vs 0.90 pH units), but the average turned out to be a worse guess for unseen regions than the statistics assumed.

1 The question

Soil pH controls which crops grow well, how much lime farmers need, and how mobile metals are in the soil. It is measured in a lab, one soil sample at a time, so maps of pH are built by predicting between and beyond sampled points.

The usual tools for this are geostatistical: Ordinary Kriging, Universal Kriging, Regression Kriging, and Gaussian Process Regression. They all use the fact that nearby samples tend to have similar values. The open question is how far that fact carries when we predict into a whole region with no samples.

Research question

Can statistics of the training data, such as sample size and spacing, nugget ratio, correlation range and trend strength, predict which kriging-based method will have the lowest error when predicting topsoil pH in geographically held-out regions of Europe?

Target

Topsoil pH measured in 0.01 M CaCl2 (unitless, about 3.3–8 here).

One row

One georeferenced agricultural topsoil sample (0–20 cm) with its measured pH.

Inputs at prediction time

Only things known at an unsampled place: its coordinates, and gridded climate and elevation. No lab measurements.

Three ideas this project puts to the test

  1. "Nearby soils are similar" stops helping at a certain distance. Beyond the correlation range, neighbours carry no information about the exact spot, and kriging falls back to an average of the samples it uses (Section 4). In our data that local average still beat the overall average far from the data, because pH changes gradually across Europe (Section 8).
  2. The usual random train/test split can flatter spatial methods. It tests at distances where neighbours are informative, unlike real gaps on a map (Sections 5 and 8).
  3. A more flexible model is not automatically better. When much of the variation is pure noise at the sampling scale (a high nugget), no method can recover it (Sections 4 and 6).

Scope of the claim. One European survey of agricultural soil (GEMAS), 26 countries. We do not claim the findings transfer to other regions, soil properties or sampling designs.

4 Nearby soils are similar, up to a point

A variogram measures how different two samples are, on average, as a function of the distance between them. Close pairs are similar (low values); far-apart pairs are as different as two random samples (the curve flattens at the sill). Two features of this curve decide how useful kriging can be:

  • the nugget: the jump at distance zero. It is variation no neighbour can explain (lab error, field-scale patchiness). We report it as a share of the sill, the nugget ratio η.
  • the range: the distance at which the curve flattens. Beyond it, neighbours carry no information.

Try it

Move the sliders. The left chart is the variogram. The right chart shows the smallest error kriging can reach, relative to just predicting the average, when the nearest sample is a given distance away. Grey lines mark the distances our two test designs actually create for GEMAS.

The maths

Model: $Z(s)=m(s)+S(s)+\varepsilon(s)$, with structured variance $C_1$, correlation $r(h)$ and nugget $C_0$. The semivariogram is $\gamma(h)=C_0+C_1(1-r(h))$ and $\eta=C_0/(C_0+C_1)$. The slider uses an exponential model, $r(h)=e^{-3h/a}$, so that $r(a)\approx0.05$.

With one neighbour at distance $d$ and a known mean, the best linear predictor has error

$$\frac{\text{MSE}_{\text{kriging}}}{\text{MSE}_{\text{mean}}}=1-(1-\eta)^2\,r(d)^2 .$$

Because the nugget $\varepsilon(s_0)$ is independent of every other sample, any predictor built from other locations has $\text{MSE}\ge C_0$, so $\text{RMSE}/\text{RMSE}_{\text{mean}}\gtrsim\sqrt\eta$. If all samples lie beyond the range, $c_0=0$ and the Ordinary Kriging system returns the generalised-least-squares mean of the samples in the system: $\hat Z(s_0)=\hat\mu$. Our models solve this system with only the 16–64 nearest samples, so $\hat\mu$ is a local mean, not the overall training mean (Section 8 shows what this does far from the data).

GEMAS fit (all cleaned samples): exponential, $C_0=0.39$, $C_0+C_1=1.10$, $\eta=0.36$, practical range ≈ 517 km. Derivations: docs/theory.md (Matheron, 1963; Cressie, 1993).

5 A random test split is easier than a real gap

To test a model we hide some samples and predict them. If we hide random samples, every hidden sample still has training neighbours about 43 km away. If we hide whole regions (500 km × 500 km blocks), hidden samples are typically 100 km and often over 200 km from the nearest training sample. That is the real situation when mapping an unsampled region.

The maths

Plugging the median test distances into $1-(1-\eta)^2r(d)^2$ with the GEMAS fit gives an RMSE ratio of 0.87 at 43 km (random split) and 0.93 at 100 km (spatial split), and 0.99 at 250 km. The random split therefore measures interpolation, the spatial split prediction into unseen regions (Roberts et al., 2017; Ploton et al., 2020). Wadoux et al. (2021) point out that spatial cross-validation can be pessimistic for whole-map accuracy; our target is held-out regions, so we use it as the main test and report the random split as a reference.

Block size: the GEMAS variograms reach 95% of the sill at 380–520 km, so we use 500 km blocks on a grid anchored at the LAEA origin, assigned to 5 folds with balanced sample counts (seed 42).

6 What the statistics predict, before testing

For each test fold we computed, from the training data and the test locations only, how much each method should improve on the average. For kriging this uses the kriging variance, which depends on where the samples are, not on their values. For trend models it uses cross-validation inside the training data. Why GPR equals kriging

With constant mean $m$, kernel $k$ and noise $\sigma_n^2$, the GP posterior mean is $\bar f(s_0)=m+k_0^\top(K+\sigma_n^2I)^{-1}(y-m\mathbf 1)$. Setting $k(h)=C_1r(h)$ and $\sigma_n^2=C_0$ gives simple kriging; estimating $m$ by GLS gives Ordinary Kriging (Rasmussen & Williams, 2006, §2.7). In theory the two differ only in how the covariance is fitted. Our code adds two practical differences: GPR uses all ~1,400 training points and subtracts the plain training mean, while OK uses the 16–64 nearest points and re-estimates the mean locally. Universal Kriging is implemented in two steps (fit a coordinate trend, then krige its residuals), an approximation to the one-step UK system.

How the models were built

  • Kriging neighbourhood. Each prediction uses the 16, 32 or 64 nearest training samples (chosen by tuning). The kriging equations are solved directly; the same solver gives the kriging variance used for the predicted skill in Section 6.
  • UK and RK. A trend model is fitted on the training fold. Its out-of-fold residuals (the trend is predicted for each sample by a model that did not see it) are used for the variogram and for kriging. This keeps a flexible trend, such as the random forest, from making the residuals look smaller than they will be at new places.
  • GPR. scikit-learn, kernel = constant × Matérn + white noise, inputs in km, targets standardised. Exact GPR is feasible with about 1,400 training samples per fold.
  • Uncertainty. Kriging and GPR also return a standard deviation for a new measurement. Section 8 checks whether 95% intervals contain about 95% of the test values.
  • No leakage. Scaling, trends, variograms, kernel parameters and the forest are fitted on the training fold only. Test values are used once, for scoring.

Tuning

Settings were chosen by cross-validation inside each training fold. The inner folds are the other outer folds, so in the spatial split the inner validation sets are whole regions, like the real test. For UK and RK the whole procedure (trend, residuals, variogram) is repeated inside each inner fold. The grids are small on purpose. The Random Forest settings are fixed (300 trees, at least 5 samples per leaf, half the features per split) and were not tuned.

8 Results and error analysis

Every number below is measured on test folds that no model saw during fitting or tuning. Skill is 1 − RMSE / RMSE of the average on the same fold: 0 means no better than the training mean, 1 would be perfect. The "spatial" split holds out whole 500 km blocks. The "random" split holds out random samples.

Why these metrics. RMSE is the main error because kriging and GPR are built to minimise squared error, and the kriging variance used for our predictions in Section 6 is a prediction of squared error. Skill divides by the error of the average on the same fold, so folds with more or less pH variation can be compared. MAE is shown as a check that is not dominated by a few large errors.

Why kriging still helps far from the data. The theory (Section 4) says that beyond the range kriging gives back the mean of the samples it uses. 9 Limitations

What the results show, and what they do not

  • Most methods are statistically close. On held-out regions the five best methods (RK (linear), UK, OK, Random Forest, GPR) are within a few hundredths of skill of each other, less than the fold-to-fold SD of 0.05 to 0.08. With 5 folds we cannot name a winner among them. This is why H1 (UK against OK) and H3 (RK is best) are inconclusive.
  • The a-priori predictions ranked the methods only roughly. The correlation between predicted and observed skill is below the pre-registered 0.7. The post-hoc check in Section 8 suggests the error scale was predicted well and the reference (the mean baseline) was not. We have not tested this explanation further.
  • The far-from-data test is thin. Only 82 test samples in the spatial split are more than 250 km from training data, and only three folds have at least 15 of them. H4 could not be decided from them.
  • Random and spatial folds are paired by number only. Fold i of one split is compared with fold i of the other, although their test sets differ. This adds noise to H6.
  • No significance tests. With five folds per split we report means, SDs and the 4-of-5 rule, nothing more.
  • Limited tuning. Grids are small, the Random Forest was not tuned, and the GPR kernel is isotropic with one length scale for all of Europe. A tuned forest may do better than the Random Forest and RK (RF) shown here.
  • The density experiment keeps discrete settings. Variogram model, neighbour count and drift degree come from the full training fold; the numeric parameters are refitted on each thinned set.

What the design limits

  • One survey, one continent, agricultural topsoil only (GEMAS, 26 countries).
  • Density is studied by thinning GEMAS, which can only make the data sparser than its 42 km spacing. We cannot say how the methods compare with denser sampling.
  • One variogram for all of Europe assumes stationarity; soils in Scandinavia and the Mediterranean may vary differently.
  • Covariates are coarse (5′ ≈ 9 km grid) and limited to climate and elevation.
  • The 500 km block size fixes how hard the test is; results at other block sizes may differ. We did not test other sizes. The size was chosen from a variogram fitted on all cleaned samples, test regions included; this affects only the split geometry, not any model.
  • Two of the supported tests were easy to pass: the nugget bound (H2) held with a wide margin because predicted skill was far below the cap, and H7(b) passed partly because the fold-to-fold spread (0.078) is large.
  • The commit history shows the hypotheses were committed and pushed first, but it cannot prove when each script was run (deviations.md, D3).
